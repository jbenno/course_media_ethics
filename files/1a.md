| Test / Rule | Content | Current status | Origin case | Key subsequent cases |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Bad tendency test** | Does the speech have a tendency to produce harmful results, even if harm is not imminent? | Abandoned — replaced by clear and present danger; never formally overruled but effectively obsolete | *Gitlow v. New York* (1925) | *Whitney v. California* (1927) |
| **Clear and present danger** | Does the speech create a clear and present danger of a specific, imminent harm that the government has the right to prevent? | Superseded by *Brandenburg* for incitement; limited residual use in narrow contexts | *Schenck v. United States* (1919) | *Abrams v. United States* (1919); *Dennis v. United States* (1951) |
| **Imminent lawless action test** | Is the speech directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action, AND is it likely to do so? Both elements required. | Current standard for incitement | *Brandenburg v. Ohio* (1969) | *Hess v. Indiana* (1973); *Counterman v. Colorado* (2023) |
| **Prior restraint doctrine** | Is the government preventing publication before it occurs? Prior restraints carry a heavy presumption of unconstitutionality and require extraordinary justification. | Active — strongest protection in First Amendment law | *Near v. Minnesota* (1931) | *New York Times v. United States* (Pentagon Papers, 1971); *NYT v. Hegseth* (2025–ongoing) |
| **Actual malice standard** | In defamation cases involving public officials or public figures: did the defendant publish knowing the statement was false, or with reckless disregard for its truth or falsity? | Active; under challenge from Justices Thomas and Gorsuch | *New York Times v. Sullivan* (1964) | *Gertz v. Robert Welch* (1974); *Hustler Magazine v. Falwell* (1988); *Dominion v. Fox News* (settled 2023); *Palin v. NYT* (2025) |
| **Public/private figure distinction** | Public figures must prove actual malice; private figures need only prove negligence. Status depends on degree of voluntary involvement in public controversy. | Active | *Gertz v. Robert Welch* (1974) | *Time Inc. v. Firestone* (1976); *Obsidian Finance v. Cox* (9th Cir. 2014); *Carroll v. Trump* (2024) |
| **Falsity burden (Hepps rule)** | In defamation cases involving matters of public concern, the plaintiff bears the burden of proving falsity — the defendant need not prove truth. | Active | *Philadelphia Newspapers v. Hepps* (1986) | Applied in *Dominion v. Fox News*; *Palin v. NYT* |
| **Unprotected categories rule** | Certain categories of speech fall entirely outside First Amendment protection: obscenity, incitement, fighting words, true threats, defamation, fraud, child pornography. Categories are fixed; government cannot create new ones by ad hoc balancing. | Active, but contested at the margins | *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire* (1942) | *United States v. Stevens* (2010); *United States v. Alvarez* (2012) |
| **Fighting words doctrine** | Words that by their very utterance inflict injury or tend to incite an immediate breach of the peace are unprotected. Narrowed significantly since 1942. | Active in theory; rarely applied in practice; no successful fighting words prosecution has reached the Supreme Court since *Chaplinsky* | *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire* (1942) | *Cohen v. California* (1971); *Snyder v. Phelps* (2011) |
| **True threats doctrine** | True threats — serious expressions of intent to commit violence — are unprotected. The mental state required of the speaker is contested. | Active; mental state requirement recently clarified | *Watts v. United States* (1969) | *Virginia v. Black* (2003); *Counterman v. Colorado* (2023) — Court held prosecution requires at least recklessness as to whether the statement is a threat |
| **Strict scrutiny (content-based)** | Does the government regulation target speech based on its content or viewpoint? If so: is there a compelling government interest, and is the law narrowly tailored to serve it? Government almost never wins. | Active — standard trigger for content-based speech restrictions | *Consolidated Edison v. Public Service Commission* (1980) | *Reed v. Town of Gilbert* (2015); *Moody v. NetChoice* (2024) |
| **Intermediate scrutiny (content-neutral)** | Does the government regulation restrict speech without targeting content (time, place, manner)? If so: is there a significant government interest, does the regulation advance it, and are alternative channels of communication left open? | Active — standard for time/place/manner restrictions | *United States v. O'Brien* (1968) | *Ward v. Rock Against Racism* (1989); *Free Speech Coalition v. Paxton* (2025) — Court applied intermediate rather than strict scrutiny to age-verification law for minors |
| **Viewpoint neutrality rule** | Government may not regulate speech because it disagrees with the speaker's viewpoint, even within a category of speech it is otherwise permitted to restrict. Absolute prohibition. | Active | *Rosenberger v. University of Virginia* (1995) | *Matal v. Tam* (2017); *R.A.V. v. St. Paul* (1992); *AP v. Budowich* (2025–ongoing) — AP argues White House access ban is viewpoint-based retaliation |
| **Overbreadth doctrine** | A law that prohibits substantially more protected speech than unprotected speech is facially unconstitutional, even if it could be constitutionally applied in some cases. | Active | *Broadrick v. Oklahoma* (1973) | *Moody v. NetChoice* (2024) — Court held plaintiffs had not shown overbreadth across sufficient range of applications |
| **Vagueness doctrine** | A law that fails to give fair notice of what speech is prohibited, or that encourages arbitrary enforcement, violates due process and is unconstitutional. | Active | *Connally v. General Construction* (1926) | *Reno v. ACLU* (1997) — Communications Decency Act struck down partly on vagueness grounds |
| **Public forum doctrine** | In traditional public forums (streets, parks), the government faces the highest burden when restricting speech. In designated public forums, similar rules apply. In non-public forums, only viewpoint neutrality and reasonableness are required. | Active | *Hague v. CIO* (1939) | *Perry Education Association v. Perry Local Educators* (1983); *Minn. Voters Alliance v. Mansky* (2018) |
| **Prior restraint / press access doctrine** | The government may not condition press access on editorial compliance; denial of access in retaliation for content is viewpoint discrimination. No absolute right of access exists, but once access is granted it cannot be selectively withdrawn on viewpoint grounds. | Contested — *AP v. Budowich* tests the outer boundary | *Sherrill v. Knight* (D.C. Cir. 1977) | *Rodríguez-Cotto v. Pierluisi-Urrutia* (2023); *AP v. Budowich* (2025–ongoing) |
| **Reporter's privilege (qualified)** | Journalists have a qualified privilege against compelled disclosure of confidential sources, derived from Justice Powell's *Branzburg* concurrence. No absolute federal constitutional privilege exists; protection depends on context and jurisdiction. | Active but structurally weak — no federal shield law exists; DOJ guidelines rescinded April 2025 | *Branzburg v. Hayes* (1972) | *In re Judith Miller* (D.C. Cir. 2005); *Natanson / Washington Post* (2026–ongoing) |
| **Free exercise / curriculum opt-out** | Parents with sincere religious objections may have the right to opt their children out of school instruction that substantially burdens religious upbringing, even if the curriculum is facially neutral. | New — unsettled downstream implications | *Mozert v. Hawkins County* (6th Cir. 1987) | *Mahmoud v. Taylor* (2025) — Supreme Court ruled 6–3 that parents may opt out of LGBTQ+-inclusive storybooks |
| **Platform editorial discretion** | Private social media platforms exercise First Amendment-protected editorial judgment when they moderate content, including through algorithms; the government may not compel them to carry speech they wish to exclude. | Active; downstream implications still being worked out | *Hurley v. Irish-American Gay Group* (1995) | *Moody v. NetChoice* (2024) — Court confirmed platforms hold editorial discretion; remanded for full analysis |
